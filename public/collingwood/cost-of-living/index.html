Collingwood Cost of Living 2026: Real Prices, Real Talk

Marcus Cole

Property Editor • Mar 16, 2026 • 9 min read

Updated 16 March 2026 | Marcus Cole reporting

Collingwood in 2026 is the suburb Melbourne can’t stop arguing about. Half the city swears it’s still the cheap, gritty inner-north option. The other half just paid $650 a week for a one-bedroom on Smith Street and is quietly having a crisis.

The truth, as usual, sits somewhere in the middle — but it’s creeping closer to “expensive” with every quarter. Collingwood’s cost of living has shifted noticeably since 2024, and if you’re thinking about moving here, already here, or just trying to figure out whether your pay rise actually kept up with your rent, this is the article for you.

I walked every major strip, priced every category, and called out the traps. No national averages. No vibes. Just numbers.

For broader context, check our Collingwood suburb page for the full neighbourhood breakdown. And if you’re comparing your options across the inner east, our Fitzroy cost of living guide, Abbotsford cost of living guide, and Richmond cost of living guide cover the same categories — the price gaps between these neighbours are worth understanding before you sign a lease.

Rent: What It Actually Costs

Let’s not sugarcoat it. Collingwood rents in March 2026 are running higher than a lot of people expect from a suburb that still carries a “working class cool” reputation.

Property Type Weekly Rent (AUD) Monthly Estimate
1-bedroom apartment $420–$540 $1,820–$2,340
2-bedroom apartment $550–$700 $2,380–$3,030
3-bedroom house $700–$920 $3,030–$3,985
Studio / room share $260–$360 $1,125–$1,560

The range is wide because Collingwood is a suburb of two speeds. The Smith Street and Johnston Street corridors — particularly the new developments and renovated warehouses — are running at the top end. Move toward the Abbotsford border or up toward Hoddle Street and you’ll find older stock at slightly less punishing rates.

For comparison, neighbouring Fitzroy is running $480–$600 for a comparable 1-bed. You’re saving maybe $40–$60 a week by being on the Collingwood side of the border. That’s roughly $2,000–$3,000 a year — not nothing, but not the massive discount it was five years ago.

The honest maths: a single person renting a 1-bed in Collingwood at $480/week is spending $24,960 a year on rent alone. You need a pre-tax income north of $70K to keep that under the 30% threshold. If you’re on the median Melbourne salary of around $85K, it’s manageable. If you’re on $60K and renting alone? You’ll need a roommate or a studio.

Groceries

Collingwood’s grocery scene is one of its genuine strengths. You’ve got a solid mix of budget and specialty options, and if you’re smart about where you shop, you can keep this category well under control.

Budget weekly shop (single, cooking at home):

  • Aldi Epping Street or the Victoria Street options just over in Abbotsford: $85–$115/week
  • Covers the basics: bread, milk, fruit, veg, chicken, pasta, rice, eggs, coffee beans

Mid-range weekly shop (couple, cooking 5 nights):

  • Aldi staples + specialty items from the Smith Street grocers and delis: $130–$170/week

Where Collingwood saves you money:

  • The Vietnamese grocers and Asian supermarkets along Victoria Street are legitimately cheaper for produce, tofu, noodles, and spices than any major chain. This is one area where Collingwood’s location pays dividends — you’re walking distance to some of Melbourne’s best value grocery shopping.
  • Queen Victoria Market is a 15-minute walk or a short tram ride. Saturday afternoon markdowns on produce are genuine bargains, not the token discounts you get at Coles.

Where it costs you:

  • The smaller independent grocers and delis on Smith Street are excellent quality but priced accordingly. A “quick top-up” at one of these places can easily hit $40–$50 for what feels like nothing.
  • If you’re doing all your shopping at Coles Smith Street (convenient but not cheap), expect to pay 10–15% more than Aldi on an equivalent basket.

Monthly grocery estimate: $340–$680 depending on household size and discipline.

Transport

Collingwood is well-connected by Melbourne’s public transport network, which keeps this category more manageable than you might expect for inner-city living.

Public transport (Myki):

  • Zone 1+2 daily cap: $10.60/day
  • CBD commute (5 days/week): roughly $53/week or $229/month at the monthly cap
  • Moderate use (3–4 days commuting + weekend trips): $35–$45/week

Key routes:

  • Tram 86 runs down Smith Street straight to the CBD — about 12 minutes to Bourke Street
  • Tram 86 also connects to Docklands and the northern suburbs
  • Bus 302 and 304 run along Hoddle Street and Victoria Parade
  • You’re a 10-minute walk from Collingwood Station on the Hurstbridge line if you need rail

Driving:

  • Parking in Collingwood is rough. Residential permit zones cover most of the suburb, and on-street parking without a permit is a lottery. Budget 15–20 minutes to find a spot most evenings.
  • Fuel: $1.75–$1.95/L at nearby servos. A full tank runs $85–$100.
  • If you’re driving to the CBD: fuel + parking ($25–$35/day) will eat $600–$800 a month. The tram is faster and a fraction of the cost.

Monthly transport estimate: $140–$230 (public transport commuter) or $500–$800 (car-dependent).

Dining Out

Collingwood is a food suburb. Full stop. But the days of cheap eats on every corner are fading. The Smith Street and Johnston Street dining scenes have matured, and prices reflect that.

The tiers:

  • Cheap eats ($12–$18): You can still get a solid banh mi, a bowl of pho, or a kebab for under $18. The Vietnamese spots along Johnston Street and the late-night options near the Abbotsford border are your friends here. See our Collingwood cheap eats guide for the full rundown.

  • Mid-range ($20–$35 per main): This is where most of Collingwood’s dining scene sits now. Pasta, Asian fusion, modern Australian — all in this bracket. Expect $25–$30 for a main, $14–$18 for entrees, $12–$16 for cocktails.

  • Date night / special occasion ($40–$65 per main): Collingwood has several restaurants firmly in fine-dining-adjacent territory. You’ll spend $150–$250 for two with wine. Check our Collingwood date night guide for specific picks.

Coffee: $4.80–$5.80 for a flat white. Collingwood’s café scene is world-class — see our best cafes and best coffee guides if quality matters more than price.

Monthly dining estimate (moderate — eating out 2–3 times/week): $350–$550.

Utilities & Bills

This one doesn’t change much based on suburb, but there are a couple of Collingwood-specific factors worth noting.

Electricity & gas:

  • Average quarterly electricity bill: $320–$420 (1–2 person household)
  • Average quarterly gas bill: $180–$280 (heating an older apartment or terrace)
  • Many Collingwood apartments are older stock with poor insulation. Your winter electricity bill will be noticeably higher than a comparable newer build in, say, Abbotsford where there’s more recent construction.

Internet:

  • NBN 50: $70–$85/month
  • NBN 100: $85–$100/month
  • Most of Collingwood has NBN coverage. Some of the older blocks still on fixed wireless will get slower speeds.

Phone plan: $30–$65/month depending on data.

Monthly utilities estimate: $280–$420 for a 1–2 person household.

Gym & Fitness

Collingwood leans toward boutique and independent gyms rather than big-box chains.

  • Budget gym (Anytime Fitness, Plus Fitness): $15–$25/week
  • Boutique classes (F45, boxing, Pilates): $30–$45/class or $180–$250/month unlimited
  • Park runs: Free. The Capital City Trail along the Yarra and through Yarra Park is one of Melbourne’s best free running routes.

Monthly fitness estimate: $60–$200 depending on your style.

The Full Monthly Budget

Here’s what a realistic month looks like for a single person in Collingwood, renting a 1-bed apartment at the median price:

Category Low Estimate High Estimate
Rent $1,820 $2,340
Groceries $340 $680
Transport (public) $140 $230
Dining out $350 $550
Utilities $280 $420
Gym $60 $200
Entertainment / misc $200 $400
Total $3,190 $4,820

That’s $38,280–$57,840 per year. To keep your housing under 30% and cover everything else comfortably, you’d want a pre-tax salary of $70K–$100K+ as a single person. A couple sharing a 1-bed or 2-bed on combined income of $130K+ would be in a much more comfortable position.

For context, if Collingwood’s numbers feel steep, the Abbotsford cost of living guide shows slightly lower rents for comparable stock, while Richmond sits in a similar bracket but offers more dining at the cheaper end.

What We Skipped and Why

We don’t include everything in these guides. Here’s what we left out and the reasoning:

Childcare costs: Every family’s situation is different, and waitlists vary wildly. If this matters to you, check the Australian Government’s Child Care Subsidy calculator and call centres directly. We’d be guessing.

Pet costs: Vet fees, pet insurance, and boarding costs vary enormously by breed and provider. Not useful to average across the suburb.

School fees: Collingwood has strong public school options (Collingwood College) and the private fees are the same as anywhere else in Melbourne. Nothing suburb-specific to report.

Health insurance, car insurance, rego: These are national/state-level costs. They don’t change because you moved from Fitzroy to Collingwood. We stick to costs that actually differ by suburb.

Tobacco and alcohol excise: Nationally set. We cover venue pricing for drinks but not retail alcohol or tobacco — that’s the same in Collingwood as it is in Mildura.

Council rates: Only relevant if you own. We cover this in our property and buying guides, not cost-of-living. Renters don’t pay council rates directly.

The Verdict

Collingwood in 2026 is no longer the cheap option it was even three years ago. The gap between Collingwood and Fitzroy has narrowed to the point where you’re choosing between them based on vibe, not budget. The real value play in this corner of Melbourne is arguably Abbotsford — lower rents, still walkable to the same amenities, and the Yarra riverfront is hard to beat.

But Collingwood offers something the data doesn’t capture well: the density of independent food, the Smith Street strip, the proximity to both the city and the northern suburbs, and a community that still feels like it hasn’t been completely homogenised. Whether that’s worth $40–$60 more per week in rent than Abbotsford is a personal call.

If you’re moving here, budget carefully. If you’re already here, audit your spending against the numbers above — most people I’ve spoken to are surprised by how much they’re overspending on groceries and transport relative to what’s actually available in the area.

The numbers don’t lie. But neither does the neighbourhood.
