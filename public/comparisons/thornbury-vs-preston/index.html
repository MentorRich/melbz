Thornbury vs Preston — Which Melbourne Suburb Wins?

J
Jack Moreno Mar 22, 2026

Thornbury and Preston sit back-to-back on the South Morang/Mernda train line, sharing High Street as their main artery. Thornbury is the more gentrified of the two — a suburb that has completed its transformation from working-class to brunch-and-natural-wine territory. Preston is still in transition, with pockets of old-school grit sitting alongside new cafés and apartment developments. The price gap between them is narrowing, but it is still meaningful. Here is how they compare.

Location and Getting Around

Thornbury sits about 7 km north of the CBD. Thornbury station is on the South Morang/Mernda line, with Flinders Street about 20 minutes away. High Street runs through the suburb with tram 86 providing an alternative route into the city. Cycling is excellent — the Merri Creek Trail runs along the suburb’s eastern edge, offering a flat, separated path into the CBD.

Preston is one stop further out. Preston station is about 22 minutes from Flinders Street on the same line. Bell station and Regent station also serve the suburb. High Street continues through Preston, and tram 86 runs its full length. The suburb is larger than Thornbury, stretching further east and north, so location within Preston matters — the western end near High Street is better connected than the eastern residential areas.

Commute to CBD: Thornbury 20 mins (train); Preston 22 mins (train).

Rent and Cost of Living

Thornbury one-bedroom rent averages around $380 per week in 2026. The suburb has gentrified significantly, and rents reflect this — period homes on the quieter streets push much higher, while smaller apartments near the station are the more accessible options.

Preston is cheaper at around $340 per week for a comparable one-bedroom. The suburb has more rental stock, including a significant amount of older apartment blocks from the 1960s-70s that keep the median down. Newer developments near the Preston Market precinct are pricier but still undercut Thornbury.

Preston Market is the biggest cost-of-living advantage either suburb has. It is one of Melbourne’s best and cheapest fresh food markets — fruit, vegetables, meat, seafood, cheese, and deli goods at prices that make supermarkets look like a scam. Thornbury residents often shop here too, but it is Preston’s asset.

Food and Coffee

Thornbury’s High Street strip has become a genuine dining destination. Restaurants like Mr Ottone (Italian), Threshold (bar and eatery), and a string of contemporary cafés have transformed the strip over the past five years. The coffee scene is strong and getting stronger, with roasters and specialty shops competing for attention. The vibe is similar to Northcote but slightly less expensive.

Preston’s food scene is more scattered but increasingly interesting. High Street Preston has a growing number of good restaurants and cafés, particularly around the intersection with Gilbert Road. The multicultural population means you can find excellent Greek, Italian, Middle Eastern, and East Asian food at neighbourhood-restaurant prices. The Preston Market precinct adds food stalls and takeaway options.

Edge: Thornbury for curated dining and coffee; Preston for multicultural variety and market-driven value.

Nightlife and Entertainment

Thornbury has a small but quality nightlife scene. The Thornbury Local, Croxton Bandroom (one of Melbourne’s best mid-size live music venues, technically on the Thornbury-Preston border), and a handful of wine bars and cocktail spots on High Street provide enough for a night out without leaving the suburb.

Preston’s nightlife is more limited. A few pubs along High Street — the Preston Hotel, the Venue — and some newer bars round out the options. The Croxton Bandroom draws people from across Melbourne for gigs, but the surrounding streets empty out quickly after shows.

Edge: Thornbury.

Parks and Green Space

Both suburbs have decent park access. Thornbury’s highlight is its proximity to the Merri Creek corridor — the trails, parklands, and semi-wild spaces along the creek are excellent for walking, running, and cycling. Penders Park and Mayer Park provide local green space.

Preston has a wider range of parks thanks to its larger footprint. Preston Reservoir’s massive open space (technically in Reservoir but accessible to northern Preston residents), T.W. Blake Park, and the Darebin Creek Trail add variety. The Preston City Oval precinct provides sports facilities and community space.

Edge: Draw. Thornbury for Merri Creek; Preston for more total green space.

Family-Friendliness

Both suburbs work for families, but Preston offers more practical advantages. The larger housing stock means more family-sized homes at accessible price points. Preston South Primary and Preston Primary are solid schools, and the suburb’s infrastructure (medical, childcare, community centres) is more developed than Thornbury’s.

Thornbury is good for families who want a walkable, café-rich lifestyle and do not mind paying more for it. Thornbury Primary has a strong reputation, and the Merri Creek provides excellent outdoor space for kids. But the housing stock skews smaller, and the gentrified commercial strips are not always family-oriented.

Edge: Preston for practical family living; Thornbury for lifestyle.

Who Lives Here

Thornbury has completed its demographic shift. Young professionals, creative workers, and couples dominate the renter population. The café-and-cycling crowd is strong, and the suburb’s character has settled into a comfortable, gentrified groove. Long-term residents from the suburb’s Greek and Italian heritage remain in family homes but are increasingly outnumbered.

Preston retains more demographic diversity. The Greek, Italian, and Middle Eastern communities that shaped the suburb are still visible, particularly around the market and the churches along High Street. Students, young families, and budget-conscious renters fill the apartment stock. Preston feels like a suburb that is still becoming something, which is part of its appeal.

Development and Future

Preston is one of the inner north’s most active development zones. The Preston Market redevelopment — a contentious project that will add significant residential and commercial space — will transform the suburb’s centre over the coming years. The level crossing removals along the Mernda line have improved the streetscape around the station. Thornbury’s development is more constrained by its smaller footprint and existing character overlays.

The Verdict

For Students: Preston wins. Cheaper rent, Preston Market for food, and only two minutes further from the CBD by train.

For Young Professionals: Thornbury wins. The curated food and bar scene, Merri Creek lifestyle, and walkable High Street strip make it the more appealing daily experience.

For Families: Preston wins. More space, lower prices, better infrastructure, and Preston Market keeps the grocery bills manageable.

For Nightlife: Thornbury wins. The Croxton Bandroom and High Street bars give it the edge, though neither suburb is a nightlife destination.

For Value: Preston wins. At $40/pw less with comparable transport, it is a clear value play — especially with the market on your doorstep.

Overall: Preston is the practical choice — more affordable, more diverse, with a market that saves you hundreds per month on groceries. Thornbury is the lifestyle upgrade — polished, walkable, and ready-made for the brunch-and-natural-wine crowd. If you are watching your budget, Preston wins. If you are willing to pay for polish, Thornbury delivers.
