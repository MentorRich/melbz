Cost of Living in St Kilda 2026: The Real Numbers

Updated 16 March 2026 | Marcus Cole reporting

St Kilda. The suburb that makes you fall in love with Melbourne and then charges you accordingly for the privilege. After eight years watching this market from the inside — as a renter, a buyer’s advocate, and someone who’s called this patch of bayside chaos home for three of those years — I can tell you this: St Kilda in 2026 is more expensive than you think, less insane than you’ve heard, and absolutely not for everyone.

But for the right person? There’s nowhere else like it.

Let’s talk numbers. Real ones. The kind your accountant would actually accept.

Rent: The Big One

Let’s not dance around it. Rent in St Kilda in 2026 runs like this:

Property TypeWeekly Rent (AUD)Annual Cost
1-bedroom apartment (inner, walkable to Fitzroy St)$480–$550$24,960–$28,600
1-bedroom apartment (outer, near Balaclava station)$420–$470$21,840–$24,440
2-bedroom apartment (Acland St / Carlisle St area)$600–$720$31,200–$37,440
2-bedroom house (Ripponlea side, near Elwood)$620–$750$32,240–$39,000
3-bedroom house (central St Kilda)$800–$950$41,600–$49,400

The gap between inner St Kilda and the Balaclava pocket is real — you can save $60–$80 a week by walking seven minutes further east. That’s $3,000–$4,000 a year, which is a holiday or six months of coffee depending on your priorities.

The houses near the Elwood border command a premium because you get the St Kilda postcode with the quieter, leafy vibe. Locals there will tell you it’s the best of both worlds. They’re not wrong.

For context, the same 1-bed in South Melbourne averages $460–$520 — comparable to St Kilda’s inner ring, but you lose the beach and the nightlife.

The salary question: To rent a 1-bed in St Kilda without losing sleep, you’re looking at needing a gross income of roughly $95,000–$110,000. For a 2-bed split between two people, $75,000 each gets you in comfortably. Below that, and you’re making real trade-offs.

Groceries: The Coles vs Aldi War

St Kilda has three major supermarkets — Coles on Fitzroy Street, Woolworths on Barkly Street, and Aldi near Balaclava Station. Plus a scattering of independents that’ll either delight your wallet or gut it depending on where you shop.

Weekly grocery costs for one person:

  • Budget (Aldi + market): $80–$100/week ($4,160–$5,200/year)
  • Mid-range (Coles/Woolies + occasional specialty): $120–$150/week ($6,240–$7,800/year)
  • Premium (whole foods, specialty stores, Acland Street Market runs): $180–$220/week ($9,360–$11,440/year)

The Acland Street bakeries and specialty stores are not where you do your weekly shop unless you enjoy financial pain. A sourdough loaf from Monarch Cakes will run you $9–$12. A bag of specialty beans from one of the local roasters, $18–$22. Beautiful? Absolutely. Economical? Not remotely.

Insider tip: The Queen Victoria Market run on Wednesday or Saturday mornings saves serious money. St Kilda locals who do it right hit Vic Market for produce and meat, Aldi for staples, and the local IGA for emergencies only.

Transport: Getting Around Without Going Broke

St Kilda is well-served by public transport, which is fortunate because parking here is a hellscape that we’ll discuss later.

Transport ModeCost (Weekly)Notes
Myki Zone 1+2 (full fare)$53.40Covers trams and trains across Melbourne
Myki Zone 1+2 (concession)$26.70Students, Health Care Card holders
Myki Zone 1+2 (senior)FreeVictorian Seniors Card holders
Tram only (St Kilda is all trams)Included in MykiRoutes 16, 96, 12 service the area
Monthly parking (St Kilda Station area)$280–$350If you need a car spot
Daily street parking (metered)$12–$18If you can find one

The 96 tram is St Kilda’s lifeline — runs straight to the CBD via South Melbourne in about 25 minutes on a good day. The 16 gets you up to the University of Melbourne. The 12 cuts across to South Melbourne and beyond.

The tram is free within the Free Tram Zone if you’re heading into the CBD from the southern edge of the zone, but St Kilda sits outside that zone. You’re Myki-ing the whole way.

Rideshare costs from St Kilda to the CBD run $18–$30 depending on surge pricing. At 2am on a Saturday, that $18 becomes $45 and suddenly that tram looks very appealing.

The parking reality: If you own a car in St Kilda, budget $300–$400/month for a secure spot. Street parking is competitive and the council is aggressive with infringement notices. One local I know racked up $800 in fines in three months before giving up and selling the car. He now uses Car Next Door when he needs one.

Dining: What Meals Actually Cost

St Kilda’s dining scene is one of Melbourne’s most varied — and one of its most price-disparate. You can eat incredibly well or incredibly expensively, and often both at the same venue.

The 2026 St Kilda dining price guide:

  • Cheap eats (Acland St pizza slices, Laksa on Carlisle): $12–$18
  • Casual dining (bistro/pub meals): $22–$32
  • Mid-range (most of Fitzroy St restaurants): $30–$45 per main
  • Date night (fine dining, degustation): $120–$250 per person
  • Coffee (flat white, regular): $4.50–$5.50
  • Beers at a local pub (pot): $8–$10
  • Cocktails at a cocktail bar: $22–$28

Coffee deserves its own mention because in St Kilda, it’s basically a utility cost. The locals go through the same ritual every morning — grab a flat white at one of the institutions on Fitzroy Street or Acland Street, pretend the $5.20 doesn’t add up to $1,898 a year, and carry on. That’s just the cost of entry.

A realistic monthly dining budget for one person:

  • Cook-at-home with occasional eating out (2–3 meals out/week): $350–$500
  • Regular eater-out (eating out 4–5 times/week): $600–$900
  • “I live on Fitzroy Street” mode: $1,200+

Utilities: Keeping the Lights On

Melbourne’s energy market in 2026 remains a competitive retail market, and St Kilda apartments — especially older ones — can be energy hogs.

Monthly utility costs for a 1–2 bedroom apartment:

UtilityMonthly Cost (AUD)
Electricity (usage + supply)$120–$180
Gas (cooking + hot water)$50–$80
Internet (NBN, 50Mbps)$70–$90
Water (usage, often included in rent)$0–$40
Total$240–$390

The big variable is gas vs electric heating. Many older St Kilda apartments still run gas heaters, which costs a fortune in winter — expect your bill to double in June–August. If you’re apartment hunting, prioritise reverse-cycle air conditioning. It’s cheaper to run and you won’t spend winter wrapped in a doona wondering about your life choices.

Internet is straightforward — most of St Kilda has decent NBN coverage. A 50Mbps plan is fine for streaming and working from home. 100Mbps will cost you $85–$110/month.

South Melbourne and Elwood have similar utility profiles. The older housing stock in those areas has the same energy efficiency issues — charming Victorian facades, terrible insulation.

Fitness: Gym Memberships

St Kilda has more gym options per square kilometre than most Melbourne suburbs. Competition keeps prices reasonable.

Gym TypeMonthly Cost
Budget (Anytime Fitness, F45 basics)$55–$75
Mid-range (plus-style clubs, specialty studios)$80–$130
Premium (boutique studios, unlimited classes)$150–$220
Pool access (St Kilda Beach Swimming Enclosure)Free
Running/cycling (the St Kilda foreshore)Free

The free option is honestly the best one in summer. Running the foreshore path from St Kilda Pier down towards Elwood Beach is one of the finest urban jogs in Australia. Winter is a different story, and that’s when the gym membership pays for itself.

St Kilda’s entertainment budget is what you make it. The beach, the Esplanade Hotel on a Sunday afternoon, and the free events on the foreshore cost nothing. A night out hitting three bars and a late-night kebab will cost you $120–$180 depending on how many cocktails you can’t say no to.

The proximity to South Melbourne Market adds cheap entertainment value too — it’s a 15-minute tram ride and you can spend a Saturday morning there for the price of a coffee and a dumpling.

That’s $38,568 to $67,920 per year just to live in St Kilda. Before tax. Before savings. Before the occasional impulse purchase or trip.

The budget scenario is doable on a $55K–$60K salary if you’re disciplined. The mid-range is comfortable at $75K–$90K. The premium tier needs $110K+ to not feel the pinch.

What We Skipped and Why

We didn’t cover childcare, because St Kilda families already know it’s brutal and the numbers are too variable to be useful in a general guide. If you’re looking at childcare in the St Kilda/Elwood/Balaclava corridor, expect $130–$165/day after subsidy.

We didn’t include pet costs, because that’s a rabbit hole of dog walkers, vet bills, and the existential question of whether your apartment is big enough for a medium-sized dog (it’s not).

We didn’t break down clothing, personal care, or subscription services because those are entirely personal. What we did cover are the fixed and semi-fixed costs that define whether St Kilda is financially viable for you — and the answer is: it’s doable, but it’s not easy, and anyone who tells you otherwise is either on a very good salary or lying.

We also didn’t factor in the cost of the occasional emotional support pastry from Acland Street, which, if you’re being honest with yourself, is at least $30/week.

The Verdict

St Kilda in 2026 is not cheap. It never has been. But it’s also not the financial ruin that the tabloids make it out to be. If you’re earning above $80K and willing to live in a 1-bed rather than chasing a 2-bed with harbour views (spoiler: there are no harbour views — this isn’t Sydney), St Kilda offers a lifestyle that most Melbourne suburbs simply can’t match.

The beach is free. The sunsets are free. The Tram 96 is not free, but it’s $4.50, and that gets you to the CBD in half an hour.

The real cost of St Kilda isn’t the dollars — it’s whether you can handle the noise, the tourists, and the seagulls that will absolutely steal your chips if you look away for two seconds.

If you can handle that, welcome home.
